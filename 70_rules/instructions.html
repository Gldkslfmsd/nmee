Examples of harmful errors: False attribution: the translation gives a person the wrong identity, gender, role, relationship, or characteristics. Examples: intended “I'd like to thank Andrea for her introductory speech” → output “…for his introductory speech.” Andrea is a woman. Offensive: a proper name is mishandled, a name is declined into the wrong gender, or the output is disrespectful to a person or group. Examples: intended “Aisha Khan” → output “ asylum con”. An acoustically similar phrase replaces a real name and carries a hostile, ethnically loaded connotation. Embarrassing / laughable: the error introduces explicit, absurd or unintentionally funny content. Examples: intended “public service media” → output “ pubic service media” Derailing / contresens: the output is shocking or unexpected, or it conveys a false or opposite message that still sounds plausible. Examples: intended “We won't end the war tomorrow” → output “We want to end the war tomorrow”. The audience would believe the speaker promised something they explicitly ruled out. Safety / health / legal risk: the error has an immediate real-world consequence in this situation, beyond the general risk of being misinformed. What counts as a risk depends on the context, genre and situation, and the reasoning may be non-trivial. Examples: intended “we are 5 km from the front” → output “we are 500 km from the front”. Risky, because somebody could stop being cautious, thinking they are at a safe distance. Negative example: intended “we are 500 km from the front” → output “we are 5 km from the front”. Somebody could be overly cautious, but it would not be a safety risk. Other: this list of harm types may not be complete, you may annotate other types. **Verification rules**

Your rule should be framed as generally as possible in testing the highlighted harm rather than hyper-specifically targeting particular wrong translations.

  • Verification rules are targeting only the highlighted harm in the translations. If you observe other than highlighted harm, do not cover it by rule.
  • Format: Write a short instruction for an AI judge, e.g. “Check whether the translation conveys that the speaker feels no inner peace.”. The rule should clearly have a pass/fail conclusion. The verification rule should be written in English regardless of the source and target languages.
  • Atomicity of your rule: Each rule should only be checking for one conceptual issue. If you find yourself listing various separable conditions into a single rule, use multiple rules instead.
  • Granularity of your rule: Your verification rule should be specific enough to catch harmful translations but flexible enough to pass any unharmful phrasing.

How to work in this editor, step by step

  1. Pick a span in the list on the left. The mark in front of it is its status: - pending, ! in progress, . finished, x remove, ? undecidable; type a mark or a word into the filter to find spans. Spans of the same segment are grouped (├─ / └─), and the header then shows span k / n.
  2. Understand the input: play the audio and read the source (gold transcript).
  3. Find the error: in the first row of the table, the reference translation, the confirmed harmful error is highlighted. Other confirmed spans of the same segment are underlined with dashes; they are separate items, write their rules there.
  4. Look at the other systems: how does each of them render the same place? This shows what can go wrong, and what correct renderings look like.
  5. Write the rules into the box, one rule per line (R1, R2, …). Rules suggested in advance may already be there: check, rewrite or delete them. Everything you type is saved automatically.
  6. Verify (button or Ctrl+Enter). The AI judge checks every translation against every rule: ✓ pass, ✗ fail, ? the AI did not answer. Hover over a tick to see the rule and the verdict. overall is ✓ only if a translation passes all rules; expected shows that the reference must fail.
  7. Check the result and improve the rules:
    • the reference must fail overall (its overall cell turns red if it does not): at least one rule has to catch the confirmed error;
    • at most two automatic translations may pass overall;
    • a rule that all or most translations pass is not useful;
    • if a verdict looks wrong to you, make the rule clearer or more precise, and verify again.
  8. Add a contrastive translation (+ add contrastive translation) if no system passes all rules: write a correct translation that should pass, verify, and check that it passes. You can also add a wrong variant to check that your rules catch it. The label in front of it can be renamed.
  9. Optional settings: reasoning lets the AI think before it answers (slower, usually more careful); ignore cache asks the AI again instead of reusing its earlier answers.
  10. Mark the span (mark as):
    • finished when the rules are done; this needs at least one rule, current verdicts, the reference failing overall, and at least one translation passing overall (a system or a contrastive one);
    • undecidable if you cannot decide whether it is a harmful error, or how to write a rule for it;
    • remove if it is not a real harmful error, or if another span of the same segment already covers it.
    Click a ticked button again to untick it.
  11. Go to the next span (next →, or Alt+↓). The status at the top (pending / in progress / finished) is updated automatically.
