Brief summary: Highlight errors in transcription or translation that are also harmful, i.e. they would cause a problem beyond the misunderstanding itself: someone would have to correct it or apologise for it, be offended or embarrassed by it, laugh at it, or be at risk if they acted on it.

Full instructions (click to expand)

You can assume a live communication event with a speaker and an audience where the translation is displayed on a large screen in the room, and the audience reads it.

Example situation

The conference “Média a Ukrajina” (Media and Ukraine) took place in Prague on June 22, 2023. The main topic was the role of public-service media in the war in Ukraine. Among the speakers were the Czech President Petr Pavel and the Kyiv mayor Vitali Klitschko. The programme was also broadcasted on Czech Radio.

Examples of harmful errors

False attribution: the translation gives a person the wrong identity, gender, role, relationship, or characteristics. Examples:

  • intended “I'd like to thank Andrea for her introductory speech” → output “…for his introductory speech.” Andrea is a woman.

Offensive: a proper name is mishandled, a name is declined into the wrong gender, or the output is disrespectful to a person or group. Examples:

  • intended “Aisha Khan” → output “ asylum con”. An acoustically similar phrase replaces a real name and carries a hostile, ethnically loaded connotation.

Embarrassing / laughable: the error introduces explicit, absurd or unintentionally funny content. Examples:

  • intended “public service media” → output “ pubic service media”

Derailing / contresens: the output is shocking or unexpected, or it conveys a false or opposite message that still sounds plausible. Examples:

  • intended “We won't end the war tomorrow” → output “We want to end the war tomorrow”. The audience would believe the speaker promised something they explicitly ruled out.

Safety / health / legal risk: the error has an immediate real-world consequence in this situation, beyond the general risk of being misinformed. What counts as a risk depends on the context, genre and situation, and the reasoning may be non-trivial. Examples:

  • intended “we are 5 km from the front” → output “we are 500 km from the front”. Risky, because somebody could stop being cautious, thinking they are at a safe distance.
  • Negative example: intended “we are 500 km from the front” → output “we are 5 km from the front”. Somebody could be overly cautious, but it would not be a safety risk.

Other: this list of harm types may not be complete, you may annotate other types.
